# Kyrgyzstan under Japarov: a research assessment

**Status as of 11 September 2026.** Independent desk research; no fieldwork, no interviews.

---

## Bottom line

Kyrgyzstan in September 2026 is a **consolidated personalist presidency with a genuinely strong economic record and a genuinely bad rights record** — and the two are not independent of each other.

Five specific judgements:

1. **The "island of democracy" framing is dead.** Every independent index that measures it now places Kyrgyzstan with its authoritarian neighbours rather than apart from them. Freedom House scores it **25/100, "Not Free"** (FIW 2026), down from 26. The 2021 constitution removed the parliamentary check; the November 2025 election removed the last party-based opposition; the February–September 2026 destruction of Kamchybek Tashiev removed the last rival inside the regime.

2. **The delivery is real and should not be dismissed.** The World Bank — not the Kyrgyz government — records real GDP growth of **9.0% (2023), 11.5% (2024), 11.1% (2025)**, budget surpluses in all three years, and poverty at the $4.20/day line falling from **16.4% to 9.1%**. Customs receipts rose roughly fivefold. Two border wars that killed hundreds were settled by treaty. These are not propaganda numbers.

3. **But the growth base is fragile and partly extra-legal.** The World Bank estimates unrecorded re-exports to Russia at **27.4% of GDP**, hidden in "Errors and Omissions." In April 2026 the EU used its anti-circumvention instrument **for the first time in its history, against Kyrgyzstan**. The World Bank names "possible secondary sanctions against Kyrgyz companies and banks" as an explicit downside risk. Growth is forecast to roughly halve in 2026.

4. **The anti-corruption campaign is better understood as a redistribution mechanism than as rule of law.** "Kusturization" recovered ~**70.7bn som (~$800m) in 2025 alone**, 96% of it through security-service cases, with no court, no published methodology, and no audit. Its own architect was then prosecuted by the same method — and served no day in prison.

5. **The system is more brittle than it looks.** The tandem that provided stability broke. Three GKNB chiefs in seven months. A southern power base without representation at the apex. A January 2027 presidential election with no meaningful competitor. Stability has been purchased by removing every mechanism through which discontent used to surface — which is exactly the configuration that preceded 2005, 2010 and 2020.

6. **Nobody can currently measure what Kyrgyz people think.** No independent national poll has been published since January 2024, the "foreign representatives" law and the end of USAID funding having removed the instruments. This is a consequence of policy, and it means claims about Japarov's popularity — including the widely-cited ~88% — are unevidenced in either direction. It also means the government is flying blind into an election.

**On "good or bad":** if the metric is state capacity, fiscal solvency, physical infrastructure and regional peace, this government outperforms every predecessor since independence. If the metric is accountable government, legal predictability, or the ability of a citizen to criticise power without criminal jeopardy, it is the worst period since 1991. Both statements are true, and any honest assessment has to hold them together.

## 1. Method, sources and limits

**What was done.** Nine parallel research streams, each reading primary and secondary sources in full: political structure, economy, human rights, foreign policy, the pro-government case, the anti-government case, political economy/corruption, society and public opinion, and a dedicated deep-dive on the 2026 rupture. Roughly 150 distinct sources were read.

**Sources deliberately balanced.** Pro-government and official: president.kg, Kabar, 24.kg, AKIpress, Ministry of Finance, Prosecutor General, Japarov's own interviews and letters, Kremlin and Xinhua readouts, Trend.az. Critical and opposition: Kloop, Temirov Live, OCCRP, RFE/RL Azattyk, Bellingcat-adjacent investigations, IPHR, Norwegian Helsinki Committee, CIVICUS Monitor, exiled bloggers. Neutral/technical: OSCE/ODIHR, World Bank, IMF, ADB, EDB, Freedom House, Bertelsmann, Transparency International, RSF, CPJ, HRW, Amnesty, UN OHCHR and the Working Group on Arbitrary Detention, Venice Commission, US Treasury, EU Council regulations, Basel Institute.

**Evidence grading used throughout:**

| Grade | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Established** | Court record, primary document, or agreement between independent sources |
| **Official claim** | Asserted by the Kyrgyz state; not independently verified |
| **Sanctioned** | An administrative determination by a government — a finding of an executive agency, *not* a court verdict |
| **Credibly reported** | Documented investigative journalism with named sourcing |
| **Alleged** | Claim by an interested party, unverified |

**Limits you should know about.**

- Several key outlets blocked automated retrieval (Eurasianet, East Asia Forum, and thediplomat.com intermittently returned HTTP 403). Where their material appears here it is corroborated via other outlets or read through syndication.
- Kyrgyz government primary sites (gov.kg, gknb.gov.kg, minfin.kg, prokuror.kg) returned 503 errors. Official positions are therefore quoted via secondary reporting of official statements, which is second-best.
- **There is no current independent polling at all.** The last published national survey is IRI's, fielded December 2023 – January 2024; Central Asia Barometer's public record ends October 2023. This is itself a finding (§9.1), not just a limitation. Treat every claim about current Kyrgyz public opinion — including the government's — as inference.
- Polling in a semi-authoritarian state suffers preference falsification in any case. Approval numbers should be read as a ceiling, not a measurement.
- The research ran up against a session-wide web-search cap partway through; later gap-filling relied on direct retrieval of already-surfaced URLs. A handful of residual gaps are listed in §11.

## 2. Who governs, and how

### 2.1 The constitutional architecture

Two referendums in 2021 built the current system. In January 2021, alongside the presidential election Japarov won with **79% on roughly 40% turnout**, 84% of voters endorsed returning from the 2010 semi-parliamentary model to a presidential one. In April 2021 a second referendum approved the new constitution on **~37% turnout with 79% in favour**.

What it did:

- Cut the Jogorku Kenesh from **120 to 90 seats**
- Gave the president appointment of judges, security chiefs and ministers **without parliamentary consent**
- Allowed **two five-year terms**
- Created the **People's Kurultai**, a 700-delegate advisory assembly with legislative-initiative rights

The **Venice Commission** and Human Rights Watch criticised the draft for undermining separation of powers. Domestically it is nicknamed the *"Khanstitution."* In **October 2023** Japarov signed a bill allowing the president to cancel Constitutional Court decisions, removing the last formal check.

ODIHR's own summary, in its final report on the 2025 election (14 August 2026), is that *"a centralised governance model was established through the redistribution of functions among state institutions, including within the judiciary, anti-corruption initiatives, and national security agencies,"* and that *"the political landscape underwent major fragmentation and the influence of political parties diminished."*

**Fusion at the top.** Since December 2024 **Adylbek Kasymaliev** has held *both* the Chairmanship of the Cabinet of Ministers and the Headship of the Presidential Administration — a combination that eliminates any independent premiership as a rival centre. The Bertelsmann Transformation Index 2026 scores **Effective Power to Govern at 1.5/10** and describes parliament as a *"rubber-stamp."*

**Centralisation below.** Municipal councils no longer elect mayors; the president appoints those of Bishkek, Osh and cities of regional significance. Village councils (*aiyl aimaks*) were cut from 452 to roughly 231. In April 2026 Japarov announced a plan to abolish oblasts and rayons entirely, replacing 44 districts and 33 cities with **20 okrugs** over three to four years.

### 2.2 The defining event: the rupture with Tashiev

This is the single most important development of the period and it happened between February and September 2026.

**Kamchybek Tashiev** — GKNB chairman from 16 October 2020, concurrently deputy chairman of the Cabinet, co-architect of the October 2020 seizure of power, and the man who bridged Kyrgyzstan's north–south divide from his Jalal-Abad base — is gone.

**What caused it.** Competing accounts, none conclusive:

- *Succession.* Japarov's own August account — self-serving but a regime admission that succession was the issue.
- *Institutional rivalry.* By 2023–24 the GKNB had expanded into economic, educational and diplomatic domains. Temur Umarov (Carnegie): *"That position brought him so much influence over the system that he was able to become a competitor to the president."* Aksana Ismailbekova (ZMO Berlin): *"There was no clear division of power… Tashiev's influence was pervasive."*
- *Assets.* OSW notes Tashiev had *"accumulated control over companies."* The Kyrgyzneftegaz material was deployed within five weeks.
- *Popularity.* Bertelsmann records Tashiev's approval at 71% against Japarov's ~88%.
- *Opportunity.* The Munich treatment created the opening.

**Why probation, not prison.** The court reasoned it was *"not a completed crime… but preparation,"* halted *"at an early stage."* Analysts read it as destroying Tashiev politically without making him a martyr — Umarov: *"Japarov doesn't want to leave this story halfway and wants to completely destroy any potential for Tashiev to come back to politics without killing him."*

**A caution against a purely persecutory reading.** Tashiev was never detained, was repeatedly among those *requesting* that the trial be closed, left court with his co-defendants in one car, was at a football match the day after conviction, and has now had the charge downgraded and confiscation lifted. That pattern is at least as consistent with a **negotiated settlement** as with a show trial. No source asserts this; it is an inference, and it is flagged as such. The Diplomat states plainly that it cannot explain the September reclassification: *"it's not clear why or how or who made that change."*

**Consequences.** Three GKNB chiefs in seven months. The security service structurally clipped — the Border Service and VIP protection moved out, the anti-corruption remit narrowed. **~500 complaints against GKNB officers** arrived at the Prosecutor General's office within two weeks of the dismissal; Asanaliyev reported **386 complaints** against GKNB officers during his time as Prosecutor General, with 18–36 criminal cases opened. The Turan Research Center counted roughly **41 MPs in northern (Japarov) networks against 42 in southern (Tashiev) ones** after the November election; CACI warns that removing the southern counterweight risks *"generating a sense of marginalization among influential southern networks"* and identifies three vulnerabilities: southern elite marginalisation, security-service groupthink, and brittle elite loyalty — with instability likely *"when the new model faces its first significant stress."*

As of 11 September 2026, **no unrest has materialised.** OSW's February judgement was that protest was possible but unlikely because *"Kyrgyz society has largely lost interest in politics."* So far that has been correct.

### 2.3 Parliament and the November 2025 election

On **25 September 2025** the Jogorku Kenesh dissolved itself — a first in Kyrgyz history — on a motion by 32 MPs carried **84–0**. Japarov called the election for **30 November 2025**.

**The system was rewritten first.** Election-law amendments signed in June 2025 (the law was amended **five times in 2025**) replaced the mixed system with a fully majoritarian **single non-transferable vote** across 30 three-member districts, one mandate per district reserved for the under-represented gender. Parties may nominate only one candidate per district and therefore **cannot win more than 30 of 90 seats**. Quotas for national minorities, youth and persons with disabilities were **abolished**. A **lifetime candidacy ban** was introduced for any criminal record — including expunged convictions — plus a non-refundable registration fee. Party nomination costs **9m som (~$104,000)**; self-nomination ~300,000 som.

| Metric | Figure |
|---|---|
| Registered candidates | 465 (ODIHR) / 476 (NHC) — 189 women (41%), 10 minority (2%) |
| Registrations **denied** | **57** |
| Parties fielding candidates | **One** |
| Registered voters | 4,294,243 |
| Turnout | **34.31% preliminary (CEC/ODIHR)**; 36.40% final (other sources). Bishkek: **26–29%** |
| Seats | 84 independents, 3 Yntymak; ~83 of 90 Japarov-aligned |
| Women | 30 (up from 20) — entirely via quota, some on **under 5%** of district vote |
| International observers accredited | 788 |
| **Citizen observer organisations** | **Two — each appointing one observer** (against 40 organisations in 2021) |

**The ODIHR verdict (final report, 14 August 2026):** the elections *"were administered efficiently but the restrictive campaign environment stifled candidate and voter engagement"*; fundamental freedoms *"are increasingly limited in practice by new restrictive legislation and its enforcement."* Election day was assessed positively in 96% of polling stations — **but the vote count was negatively assessed in over one-third of observed polling stations, and tabulation in almost half.** ODIHR found candidates *"sought to conceal their party affiliations to avoid potential negative repercussions"* and that media *"refrained from covering candidates in their news coverage or editorial programmes."* Vote-buying is recorded as *"widespread allegations"* and *"indications,"* not proven cases.

**Context around the vote.** On **27 October 2025** — five weeks before polling — courts declared **Kloop, Temirov Live and Ait Ait Dese "extremist."** On **22 November**, eight days before polling, ten people were detained under Art. 278 (organising mass unrest), including Social Democrats leader **Temirlan Sultanbekov** and **Kadyrbek Atambayev**, son of the former president; they were held until 17 January 2026. The **Social Democrats had already been disqualified in October 2025**. Results in district 13 were annulled over camera-recording of voters in 35 of 94 polling stations, all 14 candidates barred from the re-run — a blanket sanction ODIHR called disproportionate — before the CEC reversed itself and seated MPs on the original results.

### 2.4 The January 2027 presidential election

**Date: 27 January 2027** — a Wednesday. The Constitutional Court set 24 January; a law Japarov signed in late July 2026 moved the vote from the fourth Sunday to the fourth Wednesday of January. Parliament's scheduling vote slipped from 17 to **24 September 2026**; CEC chairman Tynchtyk Shaynazarov confirmed that *"even if the parliament does not adopt a resolution, the election is still deemed to have been scheduled."*

Japarov confirmed on **13 August 2026** that he will run, saying: *"Anyone who wants to run for office can do so… The elections will be transparent, and there will be no obstacles for competitors."*

Against that: the Social Democratic Party is banned and its leader in pre-trial detention; Ata-Meken was neutralised when Omurbek Tekebayev was made ambassador to Germany in 2022; Adakhan Madumarov of Butun Kyrgyzstan was removed over treason charges; Omurbek Babanov announced on 1 September 2026 that he will not run; and under 2025 legislation **anyone with a criminal record is barred from running for president** — which, if applied, excludes Tashiev and his seven co-defendants. Whether the 10 September reclassification changes that is **unresolved**.

RFE/RL noted one underappreciated feature of the February ruling: by confirming the six-year term counted as the first, the Constitutional Court also **foreclosed a term-reset that could have allowed rule to 2037**. The court has, on occasion, ruled against the executive — it also struck down the death-penalty referendum in December 2025.

## 4. The case for the government

Stated at its strongest, using independent data wherever possible.

**Stability.** Kyrgyzstan overthrew governments by street violence in 2005, 2010 and 2020, and has not done so since. Even the Foreign Policy Research Institute — a hostile source — concedes that under Japarov *"Kyrgyzstan had been more stable than at any time in its nearly 35 years"* of independence. BTI scores monopoly on force at **7/10** after the suppression of organised crime; crime boss Kamchy Kolbaev was killed by GKNB officers in October 2023 and 27 of 90 deputies elected in 2021 were removed over organised-crime ties.

**Fiscal transformation.** *(World Bank data, Established.)* Real growth 9.0% / 11.5% / 11.1% across 2023–25. **Budget surpluses all three years**, reaching 2.6% of GDP in 2025. Revenues at 39.4% of GDP. Poverty at the $4.20/day line (2021 PPP) fell **16.4% → 12.5% → 9.1%**. External debt **23.64% of GDP** at end-2025.

**Customs — the hardest number in the government's favour.** Receipts rose from **28.9bn som (2020) to 157.4bn (2025)** — roughly 5.4x — on a "Smart Customs" platform that cut clearance from five hours to sixty minutes and procedural steps from 18 to 9, **with unchanged staffing**. Tax Service revenues rose from 78bn to over 300bn som. Bertelsmann — no friend of the government — credits the tax and customs digitalisation with *"drastically increasing state revenues"* and shrinking the shadow economy. Money that used to be stolen at the border is now in the budget.

**Digital state.** The **Tunduk** platform went from 363,000 transactions in 2018 to **3.5 billion in 2024**, with 165 digitised services, saving citizens an estimated **21 million hours and 1.7bn som** in 2024 alone. Every certificate a citizen no longer has to fetch is a bribe a clerk cannot extract — the most credible petty-corruption claim the government makes.

**Pass-through to households.** Teachers' and medical workers' salaries **doubled from 1 April 2026** (26,000 → 52,000 som). Civil servants received up to 50% from September 2026, the lowest grades up 74%. Pensions indexed from October 2026. Real wages rose **15.3%** in H1 2026. BTI independently confirms **100+ new school buildings** built 2022–2024.

**Megaprojects that were talk for thirty years are now concrete.** **Kambarata-1** (1,860 MW, 256m dam, $4.2bn) has a signed tripartite financing model — Kyrgyzstan 34%, Uzbekistan and Kazakhstan 33% each — with World Bank technical assistance. The **China–Kyrgyzstan–Uzbekistan railway** is under construction: 530 km total, ~304 km in Kyrgyzstan, 29 tunnels, 50 bridges, the first tunnel completed September 2026.

**Borders — the clearest unambiguous win.** With **Uzbekistan**, the treaty was signed November 2022 and ratified January 2023, ending a 30-year dispute. With **Tajikistan**, after clashes in 2021 and 2022 that killed well over a hundred people, the **Treaty of Khujand was signed 13 March 2025** and ratified within weeks, delimiting the entire ~970km border for the first time ever — equal-area land swaps, joint water management, a commitment not to fly drones or station heavy equipment along the border. Crossings reopened, flights resumed, and the tri-junction with Uzbekistan was settled on 31 March 2025. By July 2026 nearly **90% of engineering work was complete**. The UN Secretary-General publicly welcomed it. **No armed clashes have been reported since.** This required politically costly land swaps a weaker leader could not have survived.

**International standing.** On **3 June 2026** Kyrgyzstan won its first-ever **UN Security Council seat** (2027–28), beating the Philippines **142–49** after four rounds. On 1 September 2026 Bishkek hosted the **SCO's 25th-anniversary summit** — 21 heads of state, the Bishkek Declaration plus 27 further documents — alongside a Xi Jinping state visit, the 35th independence anniversary and the World Nomad Games.

**The government's own rebuttal to Western criticism** deserves to be stated in its own words. Japarov's February 2024 letter to Secretary Blinken on the foreign representatives law: the US letter shows *"signs of interference in the internal affairs of our state"*; the bill is *"close to the current Foreign Agents Registration Act (FARA) adopted in the U.S.A."*; *"**Why is it allowed for you, but not for us?**"*; *"Society and the state must see where, from what foreign sources and for what purposes the funding is coming from."* The FARA parallel is imperfect but not frivolous, and the sovereignty argument lands with domestic and Global South audiences. On media, Japarov told Kabar in January 2026: *"Law enforcement agencies don't detain people for speaking out or criticizing. They detain those who, under the guise of freedom of speech, secretly engage in activities aimed at destabilizing the country."*

## 5. The case against

**Authoritarian consolidation is documented, not inferred.** See §2.1 and §3. The specific mechanism — presidential appointment of judges, the 2023 power to cancel Constitutional Court rulings, the 87–0 confirmation of a security chief with no questions asked — is a system without checks.

**Selective justice, demonstrated by direct comparison.** Kloop cameramen **Aleksandr Aleksandrov** and **Joomart Duulatov** received **five years in prison** for filming. The former security chief convicted of plotting a coup **served no day in custody, kept his property, and had the charge downgraded**. Whatever the merits of either case, the same legal system produced both outcomes in the same twelve months.

**"Kusturization" is extraction, not law.** Prosecutor General Asanaliyev told parliament on 7 April 2026 that **70.68bn som** was recovered in 2025, of which **67.9bn (96%) came from national-security-agency cases** — roughly **$800m in one year**, through a mechanism with **no statutory basis, no court, no published methodology for assessing damages, and no audit**. BTI finds seized property is *"typically deposited with the Office of Assets Management rather than legitimate state property agencies, with limited public disclosure of subsequent disposition,"* and that *"by late 2024, no medium-sized or especially large businesses with assets large enough to confiscate were safe."*

**Where the assets landed.** *(Credibly reported — OCCRP.)* Tursuntai Salimov's Madina market went to **Tarim Trade**, owned by the son of Khabibula Abdukadyr — head of the same trading family identified in 2019 as the beneficiary of the customs scheme Matraimov allegedly protected. A sports centre passed to a company co-owned by a friend of the president and **the wife of the president's brother**. A concrete-factory stake went to an ally of the head of the Presidential Administrative Directorate. This reads as transfer between private owners with the state as intermediary — not recovery to the fisc.

**The Matraimov precedent.** *(Sanctioned + Established.)* Designated under Global Magnitsky in December 2020 over allegations he protected a scheme moving **$700m+** out of the country. Extradited from Baku **26–27 March 2024**; released November 2024 after paying **$200 million**; on 6 December 2024 convicted of **illegal border crossing and hooliganism and fined 100,000 som (~$1,150)**. On **30 August 2026**, despite a travel ban, he staged his son's wedding at the Marriott in Tashkent with Philipp Kirkorov performing. This is the clearest single datum on whether kusturization is punishment or price.

**The Presidential Affairs Directorate as a parallel cabinet.** *(Credibly reported.)* Under **Kanybek Tumanbaev** it controls 30+ subordinate organisations including mortgage construction, population registration, passport and banknote printing, and control of international internet traffic. Revenue rose from **770.9m som (2021) to 14.138bn (2025) — 18-fold — with 25bn projected for 2026**, reporting only to the president. In **April 2022 Japarov eliminated competitive procurement requirements for state-owned enterprises**; previously public budget data was restricted. OCCRP identified at least **11 state projects, six worth ~$137m**, awarded to five interlinked companies whose owners connect to Japarov or Tumanbaev. On 23 July 2026 Kloop reported Tumanbaev **holds a valid Russian passport**, which under Kyrgyz law bars him from senior state office; as of 9 September no response had come in over 40 days.

**A state that cannot cost its own flagship project.** On 8 September 2026 presidential press secretary Askat Alagozov said the **Bishkek Arena cost $386 million**, against Tashiev's 2024 public figure of **$60–70 million**. A commission was convened to establish whether the real figure lies between $60m and $500m.

**Repression, in numbers and names.** RSF rank down 64 places since 2020. HRW documents **100+ politically motivated arrests since 2021**. The **UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention** found violations in the cases of Makhabat Tazhibek kyzy, Rita Karasartova and the Kempir-Abad women. A December 2025 **UN OHCHR survey of 115 CSOs** found **77% self-censoring**, over **90%** of human rights groups, **37% downsizing**, **7% self-liquidating** — and of 62 women's organisations, four closed and over 40% moved away from rights work. Amnesty's single most damning statistic: **only two domestic organisations monitored the November 2025 elections, against 40 in 2021.**

**Gold opacity.** Kyrgyzstan officially reported **17.8 tonnes / $1.4bn** of gold exports in 2024; TradeMap mirror data from partner countries shows **70 tonnes / $5.9bn** — a ~52-tonne gap. The government's explanation is technical and partly legitimate: monetary gold held as central bank reserves is excluded from external trade statistics under EAEU classification. That is plausible and could not be tested. But it means roughly **three-quarters of Kyrgyz gold leaving the country is invisible in the country's own trade accounts.**

**The China trade gap.** Chinese customs recorded **$27.2bn** in bilateral trade in 2025 against Kyrgyzstan's **$4.97bn** — a gap of nearly **$23bn**, up from $17.1bn in 2024. Kyrgyz customs attribute it to transit accounting; traders told RFE/RL that undervaluation, misclassification and absent invoices explain much of the rest. Japarov has publicly cited the *Chinese* figure.

**Governance failures the government does not advertise.** An **energy emergency declared 1 August 2023 runs to 31 December 2026**; hydropower supplies ~90% of generation and the deficit was projected to reach 5–6 billion kWh by 2026. Bishkek goes dark at 10pm to save electricity, and a cascade blackout hit four Central Asian states on 14 August 2026. Registered domestic violence cases rose **35%** in H1 2025 to **10,164**.

Population **7.3m**; GDP **$22.6bn**; GDP per capita **$3,086** (2025).

Growth in 2025 came from private consumption (+17.4% real), investment (+15.9%) and services (+16.4%, mainly wholesale/retail and construction). Through **January–July 2026 growth was running at 11.1%** — well above the World Bank's 6.1% full-year forecast — with fixed-capital investment up **58.8%** and real wages up **15.3%**.

**Two things to notice in that table.**

First, the **employment rate is essentially flat** — 62.8% to 63.4% — across three years of double-digit growth. Whatever is producing the growth is not producing jobs at the same rate.

Second, **net FDI is 3% of GDP and falling**. An economy genuinely re-rating its investment climate would not look like this.

### 6.2 Why the base is fragile

**Re-exports.** The World Bank states that the current account deficit *"remains elevated due to under-reporting of re-exports, which continue to be captured under 'Errors and Omissions', and are estimated at **27.4 percent of GDP**."* OSW attributes the rise in GDP from $12.27bn (2022) to $20.16bn (2025) *"largely to increased Russian cooperation and sanctions circumvention."*

**Remittances.** Averaged ~30% of GDP 2012–21; **19.9% of GDP** in 2025 (up 17.9% year-on-year). But the migrant stock in Russia fell from ~**854,000** (2022) to **502,242** (Kyrgyz Labour Ministry, 2026) as Russia securitised migration. Moscow proposes a four-year experiment from 1 January 2027 tying migrants to a named employer, job and region, with mandatory genomic registration.

**The fiscal surplus was partly a one-off — but the pessimistic forecast is being beaten.** The World Bank is explicit that 2025 revenues rose *"driven by a large Central Bank's profits transfer,"* and projected a 2026 flip to a **−3.5% deficit** on slower activity, a smaller central bank transfer, and the wage increases the government is celebrating. The IMF separately urged Kyrgyzstan to **suspend profit transfers to the budget until central bank capital is restored**.

In the event, **budget revenues for January–May 2026 came in at 376.3bn som (~$4.3bn), up 44.1% year-on-year**, with 2026 reporting describing a surplus rather than a deficit. On current data the World Bank's deficit forecast looks likely to be wrong. This cuts *for* the government and should be recorded as such — though it also means the underlying activity is running hotter than the IFIs modelled, which is the overheating problem, not its absence.

**Overheating.** The IMF's Article IV mission (18 March–1 April 2026) found *"signs of overheating that requires timely policy recalibration"* — inflation above the 5–7% target, rapid credit expansion, strong wage growth, high liquidity, elevated NPLs. The Executive Board concluded the consultation on **4 June 2026** (Press Release 26/185); this was **surveillance, not a financing programme**, and no new IMF programme was found to be in prospect. The policy rate was raised through 2025 to **11%** (last documented move, November 2025; no 2026 change could be confirmed). Inflation was **11.5% in July 2026, 11.7% by 24 August** — roughly double target — and the **year-end forecast was revised up from 9% to 14–15%**. The som is tightly managed and nominally stable at **87.45/USD** on 11 September 2026.

**Reserve concentration is a live vulnerability.** Gross international reserves hit a record **$10.18bn in January 2026**, up 96% year-on-year from $5.19bn — then **fell to $8.69bn by March, a 14.6% drop driven entirely by gold revaluation**. Gold was **76.5% of reserves**, worth $7.94bn at January's peak gold price and $6.66bn in March — a paper loss of about **$920m in two months**. NBKR holdings stood at 1,512,392 troy ounces (~47 tonnes) at 31 August 2026. The National Bank itself concedes that *"the high concentration of gold makes the economy vulnerable to commodity price volatility."* Headline reserve strength and headline reserve fragility are the same fact.

**Forecast dispersion is itself a warning.** For 2026: World Bank **6.1%**, ADB **8.9%**, EDB **10.2%**. A four-point spread for the same year signals genuine uncertainty about what the measured activity actually is.

**Structural.** The World Bank's verdict: Kyrgyzstan *"remains one of the most economically vulnerable countries in the Europe and Central Asia region"*; growth and the current account *"continue to rely on gold exports and remittances"*; productivity and jobs are constrained by *"the weak business environment, the privileged role of state-owned enterprises, and high tax compliance costs."* And: *"The government has been expanding the state presence in the economy by creating new state-owned enterprises."*

### 6.3 Sanctions exposure — the single largest tail risk

This is where the state stopped being an alleged beneficiary of shadow flows and became, on its own account, the operator of them.

**Designations (Sanctioned):**

- **15 Jan 2025** — US Treasury designates **Keremet Bank** for facilitating cross-border payments for Russia's Promsvyazbank, noting the Kyrgyz Finance Ministry had sold a controlling stake to a firm linked to a Russian oligarch, and that the purchase *"was intended to create a sanctions evasion hub."*
- **14 Aug 2025** — Treasury sanctions **Garantex**, successor exchange **Grinex**, and Kyrgyz-registered **Old Vector**, issuer of the rouble-backed **A7A5** stablecoin created for A7 LLC, owned by sanctioned Moldovan oligarch **Ilan Shor** and Promsvyazbank.
- **20 Aug 2025** — UK sanctions **Capital Bank**, its director **Kantemir Chalbayev**, Old Vector, TengriCoin and Altair Holding, citing **$9.3bn** moved via A7A5 in four months. Chainalysis put cumulative A7A5 volume above **$51bn** through July 2025; an RFE/RL investigation estimated $79bn.
- **Oct 2025** — the EU's **19th package** designates **Tolubay Bank** and **Eurasian Savings Bank** for circumventing EU sanctions.
- **23 Apr 2026** — the EU's **20th package** designates **Keremet** and **Capital Bank**, bringing **four Kyrgyz banks** under EU transaction bans, and freezes the assets of **CJSC TengriCoin**. Several Kyrgyz logistics companies were barred from European ports and transport networks.

**And the decisive one.** On **23 April 2026** the EU activated **Article 12f of Regulation 833/2014 — its anti-circumvention instrument — for the first time in its history, against Kyrgyzstan.** Council Regulation (EU) 2026/506 banned exports of CN 8457 10 (metal-working machining centres, used for missile and drone components) and CN 8517 62 (communications equipment, used for field networks and drone telemetry). The Council found Kyrgyzstan had *"systematically and persistently"* failed to prevent re-export. Its cited data: EU high-priority-item exports to Kyrgyzstan up **~800%**, Kyrgyz exports of those items to Russia up **~1,200%**, over the first ten months of 2025. **No transitional period applied.**

**Japarov's defence is itself the most damaging document.** He told Kabar in August 2025 that *"neither the UK nor the U.S. has provided a single fact of violation… such facts simply do not exist"* and that sanctions were *"politicised."* But he justified the arrangement this way:

> *"To prevent any of them from falling under sanctions, we decided that only the state-owned Keremet Bank would work with the Russian rouble… All operations are under state control, and the income goes directly to the state treasury."*

That is a presidential statement that the sanctioned channel was **state policy and state revenue**.

**Behaviour then changed.** On ~21 May 2026 the Justice Ministry ordered **50 companies** to cease activity over "increased sanctions risks" — **naming none of them**; a sanctions envoy was appointed in June; Eldik Bank and ABank cut off 130+ companies; a further **19 liquidations** were ordered on 18 August 2026, also unnamed. Erica Marat's assessment: the shutdown *"is absolutely a fear of being sanctioned,"* not moral judgement.

**Already lost: the garment sector.** Russia began enforcing EAEU tariffs and ending grey "cargo" schemes in 2025 — 20% import VAT, Russian-accredited certification, Chestny Znak marking, bank-only settlement. Piece rates fell from 200 to 120 som; t-shirts to 40–60 som. *"Workshops are closing by the thousand,"* a Bishkek workshop manager told Kloop. This is what the end of arbitrage looks like in one sector, and it has already happened.

### 6.4 Kumtor

*(Established.)* The Centerra settlement closed **29 July 2022**: Centerra purchased and cancelled all 77,401,766 shares held by Kyrgyzaltyn, paid ~**$85m** cash, and transferred 100% of its two Kyrgyz subsidiaries and the mine to Kyrgyzaltyn and the Republic, which assumed all reclamation obligations. No admission of liability either side.

Kumtor is now the fiscal engine and the political alibi. Japarov said on 14 August 2026 that revenue since 2021 reached **$5.1bn** with net profit above $2bn, spent on housing, 500 schools, roads and pensions *(Official claim)*. In H1 2026 Kumtor transferred **19bn som** to the budget on revenue of $732.7m and net profit of $418.9m, and paid a **$300m dividend**. Underground mining began 27 August 2025.

Against that, three things:

**Output is falling.** 17.3 tonnes (2022) → 13.5t (2023) → 12.2t (2024) → **12.1t (2025)**. Ore grades have collapsed from about **5 grams per tonne in 2015 to under 1 g/t** now. Underground mining and tailings reprocessing are intended to offset this; the World Bank nonetheless forecasts 2027 growth easing *"due to a decline in gold production."*

**The state's take fell even as gold prices rose.** Kumtor's taxes and mandatory payments to the state were almost **$388m in 2022, $196m in 2023 and roughly $185m in 2024** — less than half, in a rising gold market. Eurasianet's headline on this is "Gold mine no cash cow for Kyrgyz government," and it is the most awkward single fact for the nationalisation case.

**Oversight.** Parliament revoked the 2019 decree directing Kumtor profits to regional development and environmental protection, and of $67m transferred by Centerra, reportedly **not a single environmental project was financed**. No independent audit or parliamentary oversight of the spending claims was located in any source.

### 6.5 Crypto: a sector larger than the economy

*(Established, from the Financial Market Regulation and Supervision Service.)* In 2025 turnover through licensed virtual-asset service providers reached **2.73 trillion som across more than 2.12 million transactions — roughly $31bn**, against GDP of ~$22.6bn. Up to 90% of volume is simple conversion, predominantly into and out of USDT. Kyrgyzstan registered **200+ crypto exchanges and exchange operators** plus 11 industrial mining companies, and jumped from **76th to 19th** in Chainalysis' Global Crypto Adoption Index between 2024 and 2025. Crypto generated about **$22.8m in tax revenue in 2025** — more than Dordoi Bazaar (~$7.9m) and all patent-based businesses (~$13.6m) combined.

The state is not a bystander. In October 2025 Japarov, alongside **Binance founder Changpeng Zhao — appointed presidential adviser on digital assets** — launched the national stablecoin **KGST**, pegged 1:1 to the som on Binance's BNB Chain, announced legal recognition of a **digital som** CBDC, and established a **national crypto reserve including BNB**. Separately, the state-owned Virtual Asset Issuer under the Finance Ministry launched the gold-backed **USDKG**.

A sector turning over more than national GDP, in a jurisdiction whose largest crypto issuer has been sanctioned by the US, UK and EU, is not a fintech success story on its own terms. It is the same exposure as §6.3, in a different wrapper.

## 7. Rights, media and civil society

### 7.1 The legal architecture of control, 2024–2026

| Instrument | Date | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Foreign representatives law** | In force 15 Apr 2024 | Foreign-funded NGOs in "political activity" must register; suspension up to 6 months or court-ordered liquidation. Venice Commission: *"real risk of stigmatising, silencing and eventually eliminating"* critical NGOs |
| Libel/insult recriminalised | Feb 2025 | Ministry of Culture may fine up to 200,000 som (~$2,000) **without judicial approval** |
| Religion law | Jan 2025 | 500+ members per region to register; bans unregistered activity, proselytism, and clothing *"that makes it impossible to identify a person"* |
| "False information" amendments | Jul 2025 | 20,000 som (individuals) / 65,000 som (outlets) |
| Extremism amendments | Jul 2025 | **Possession** of extremist materials recriminalised (up to 3 yrs); online incitement 3–5 yrs |
| **Mass Media law** | Passed 25 Jun, signed 6 Aug 2025 | Mandatory registration incl. online; foreign ownership capped at **35%**; freelance journalism eliminated; **three Justice Ministry warnings in a year triggers compulsory liquidation**. Mandatory registration had been dropped in the January draft and **reinserted at second reading**. Implementing regulations adopted 10 Apr 2026 |
| Digital Code | 18 Jun 2025 | Personal data, telecoms, AI |
| Assembly law amendments | Tabled Mar 2026 | Would let local authorities set protest venues and routes **without court approval** |

The European Parliament *"strongly regretted"* the Media Law in resolution P10_TA(2025)0171 and has linked human-rights compliance to ratification of the EU–Kyrgyzstan Enhanced Partnership and Cooperation Agreement and to GSP+ status. **No formal GSP+ review or withdrawal procedure was found to have been opened.**

### 7.2 Media

**Temirov Live / Ait Ait Dese.** Eleven journalists tried together; on 10 October 2024 four convicted, seven acquitted. Director **Makhabat Tazhibek kyzy** — six years; presenter **Azamat Ishenbekov** — five; two others three years' probation. Upheld December 2024; Supreme Court rejected cassation 25 February 2025. Ishenbekov pardoned April 2025. The **UN WGAD found Tazhibek kyzy's detention arbitrary in October 2025**; the Supreme Court ordered review in March 2026 and she was **released 23 March 2026 under a travel ban** after 18 months. Her **retrial opened 26 August 2026**; charges stand, and on 26 August a court refused to let her travel to receive a Clooney Foundation award. Her husband **Bolot Temirov**, the founder, was stripped of citizenship and deported in 2022 — **after investigating Tashiev's family**.

**Kloop.** Liquidation upheld 16 July 2024. GKNB raids 28 May 2025. On 17 September 2025 four convicted under Art. 278; cameramen **Aleksandr Aleksandrov** and **Joomart Duulatov** got five years, reduced on 25 November 2025 to three years' probation and released. Co-founder **Rinat Tukhvatshin** is in exile; Kyrgyzstan sought an **Interpol Red Notice in October 2025, which Interpol rejected**.

**The extremist designation.** On **27 October 2025** a court designated **Kloop, Temirov Live and Ait Ait Dese** — and Temirov and Tukhvatshin personally — as extremist. The **Supreme Court upheld it in March 2026**. Sharing or liking their material can now constitute support for an extremist organisation: dissemination 3–5 years (up to 7 aggravated), possession up to 3 years after written warning. It is the first time Kyrgyzstan has so designated news outlets. CPJ called it *"a shocking and desperate attempt to stifle anticorruption reporting into the top echelons of Kyrgyzstan's government."*

**Aprel TV** was ordered closed on 9 July 2025, the court accepting that its *"sarcasm, mimicry and expressive speech"* undermined state authority — one prosecution expert testified that *"those who live in rural areas and shepherd boys"* might mistake its satire for fact.

**Azattyk (RFE/RL).** Blocked October 2022, licence revoked April 2023, block reversed July 2023. In **February 2025 Japarov publicly endorsed Elon Musk's call to shut RFE/RL**, saying *"People do not need Azattyk"* and calling its mission obsolete. Its current registration status under the 2025 media law could not be established.

**State media.** NTRK was nationalised in 2022; its Director General is appointed and dismissed by the president — and was replaced in **October 2025, during the election campaign**.

### 7.3 Prosecutions of critics

A partial list, all Established as to charge and outcome:

- **Rita Karasartova** — five years' non-custodial restricted freedom plus fine, September 2025, for sharing an activist's letter on Facebook; appeal rejected 11 December 2025. UN WGAD called it *"recycling"* of her earlier Kempir-Abad charges.
- **Temirlan Sultanbekov** (Social Democrats leader) — vote-buying conviction April 2025 (three years' probation) after a 100-day hunger strike; **re-detained days before the November 2025 election** under Art. 326; trial opened behind closed doors in mid-2026. On 10 September 2026 he wrote to Japarov: *"Give me 20 years or life imprisonment — I don't care. But hold an open trial."*
- **Ravshan Jeenbekov**, former MP — seven years eight months, June 2025. Ex-president **Almazbek Atambayev** — 11 years in absentia, June 2025.
- **Ernis Zheentaev**, media designer — **six years, 13 February 2026**.
- **Kanykei Aranova** — 3.5 years over Facebook posts she says came from a fake account; Supreme Court rejected reconsideration August 2026.
- **Mavlyan Askarbekov** — detained 12 June 2026 for criticising **Chinese business influence**; trial opened 20 August 2026.
- **Azamat Bayamanov** — detained 24 July 2026 over videos opposing **land allocation to foreign investors**.
- **Aziza Abdurasulova**, human rights defender — detained June 2026 after supporting Askarbekov.
- Lawyers were targeted too: **Samat Matsakov**, who defended Temirov Live journalists, was arrested November 2024 and released February 2025 under a travel ban; the bar association objected that only the Prosecutor General may open a case against a lawyer for professional activity.

**Kempir-Abad — the counter-example.** Twenty-plus politicians and activists were arrested in October 2022 over opposition to the Kempir-Abad reservoir transfer to Uzbekistan. Prosecutors sought up to 20 years. On **14 June 2024 all 22 were acquitted** for lack of evidence of a crime, and on **27 March 2026 Bishkek City Court upheld the acquittals**, releasing those under house arrest. This matters: the courts do not invariably deliver what the prosecution asks.

**An official targeting list.** On **20 July 2026 Daiyrbek Orunbekov**, head of the presidential administration's information policy service, published a Facebook post naming and picturing **75+ activists, journalists, lawyers and opposition figures**, threatening prosecution for "fake news" about China, land use and foreign debt. Some named said they had never posted about China; concerns were raised that the photographs came from the state digital services platform. The Interior Ministry opened a preliminary investigation in August 2026 into unlawful publication of personal data.

### 7.4 Assembly, torture, minorities

**A blanket court-ordered ban on protests in central Bishkek has run continuously since spring 2022** — originally at the Russian embassy's request to stop anti-war protests. It covers Ala-Too Square, the presidential and parliament buildings, the Supreme Court and the Russian embassy; protesters are directed to Gorky Park. Official state and municipal events remain exempt on Ala-Too Square. It has been extended repeatedly, most recently through **16 September 2026**. A comparable ban has applied in central Osh since July 2024.

**The National Centre for the Prevention of Torture — the OPCAT national preventive mechanism — was abolished** by a law parliament passed 25 June and Japarov signed **23 September 2025**, its mandate transferred to an Ombudsperson's office still under development. The UN High Commissioner said torture prevention was *"seriously undermined."* Pastor **Pavel Schreider** reported being beaten by five GKNB officers including with an iron pipe, and was diagnosed with traumatic brain injury; five UN special rapporteurs raised his case. He was sentenced to three years in July 2025.

**LGBT.** The anti-"propaganda" law was signed August 2023. In April 2025 police entered an apartment housing **five transgender women without a warrant**, allegedly beat them, and conducted non-consensual genital examinations; a court convicted them of minor hooliganism, five days each. An **anti-trans bill passed first reading 4 June 2026** that would end legal gender recognition and ban gender-affirming care; UN experts found it incompatible with Kyrgyzstan's obligations on 15 May 2026. At the 2025 UPR Kyrgyzstan **rejected all LGBTI-related recommendations**, citing "traditional values."

**Ethnic Uzbeks.** In April 2025 Osh authorities demolished around **200 properties on Navoi Street** — a predominantly Uzbek street worst hit in the 2010 violence and rebuilt with international support — without adequate consultation or compensation. CERD criticised the Osh redevelopment plans in June 2025 for omitting reconstruction of traditional Uzbek neighbourhoods.

**Death penalty.** Japarov proposed a referendum to reinstate capital punishment after a child's rape and murder; the **Constitutional Court ruled in December 2025 that it would be unconstitutional**, and the proposal stalled.

### 7.5 The "thaw" that did not happen

After Tashiev's dismissal a presidential spokesperson indicated law enforcement would make fewer arrests during investigations and emphasise *"prophylactic measures."* Officials stressed this did not mean tolerance of criticism. Monitors' verdict for March–August 2026 is that **repression persisted**: new cases opened (Askarbekov, Bayamanov, Abdurasulova), protest restrictions extended, the extremist designations upheld, an official targeting list published.

There were genuine counter-currents in the same period, and an honest account has to record them: the Kempir-Abad acquittals upheld; Tazhibek kyzy released; pardons for Ishenbekov, Torokulova and Myktybek; Mamyrkulova partially acquitted on appeal; Bayan Jumagulova acquitted on appeal in June 2026; the death-penalty referendum blocked; an investigation opened into Orunbekov's targeting post.

Japarov's own framing, in an August 2026 interview, is that court rulings may occasionally reflect politics but that the share of politically influenced cases is **"0.001 percent."**

## 8. Foreign policy and external position

**Russia — dependence with friction.** Remittances at ~20% of GDP; the Kant airbase leased to 2032 with a joint air-defence component; S-300PS and Tor-M2KM delivered April 2025; Gazprom contracts through 2040; ~1,700 Russian companies operating. Putin made a state visit 25–27 November 2025 and returned for the SCO summit on 1 September 2026. Two 2026 ratifications deepen entanglement: the $200m Kyrgyz-Russian Slavic University campus (2 May) and, notably, **an agreement on the status of Russian Interior Ministry representative offices in Kyrgyzstan (26 July)**. Russia is funding nine Russian-language secondary schools due to open in 2027 — analysts read this explicitly as targeting the next generation.

The friction is over migrants. The **Moscow bathhouse raid of 10 April 2025** — ~60 Kyrgyz nationals, eyewitness accounts of beatings and torn documents over five hours — produced a summons of the Russian ambassador and a formal note, but no rupture. Ukrainian strikes on Russian refining caused fuel shortages in Kyrgyzstan in mid-2026.

**China — scale and opacity.** Roughly a third of foreign trade, nearly 40% of imports, and the ~$23bn statistical gap noted above. More than 40% of official external debt was owed to China in 2022; Kyrgyzstan has since legislated a ceiling barring more than 45% of external debt to any single creditor. Xi's state visit (30 August–1 September 2026) produced a joint declaration and a bilateral treaty on permanent good-neighbourliness. **Public sentiment is a live constraint**: a 2023 dataset counted 192 China-related protests across Central Asia, concentrated in Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan, driven by nationalism, environmental damage and Xinjiang — and, as §7.3 shows, criticising Chinese influence is now itself prosecutable.

**The West — transactional and cooling on mobility.** Kyrgyzstan appeared on a June 2025 US list of 36 countries given 60 days to meet State Department benchmarks; from **21 January 2026 immigrant visa processing was frozen** for applicants from 75 countries including Kyrgyzstan, with B1/B2 bonds up to **$15,000**. Kyrgyzstan cut US visa-free stays from 60 to 30 days effective 1 January 2026, and Deputy PM **Edil Baisalov** — who called for reviewing visa-free access for Americans as *"a matter of parity and mutual respect"* — was then appointed **ambassador to Washington** in May 2026. At the 6 November 2025 C5+1 summit, Kazakhstan signed ~$17bn in deals and Uzbekistan a trade agreement; **Kyrgyzstan came away with no new financing instruments.**

With the EU: the Enhanced Partnership and Cooperation Agreement was signed 25 June 2024 but ratification is linked by the European Parliament to human-rights conditions; GSP+ removes tariffs on two-thirds of Kyrgyz exports, conditioned on 27 conventions. The EU is simultaneously Kyrgyzstan's trade benefactor and the author of the first-ever anti-circumvention designation against it.

**Turkey.** The Gülen file closed in Ankara's favour: on 31 December 2024 Kyrgyzstan handed the **Sapat** school network — 20 schools, 11,000+ students, founded 1992 — to the Turkish Maarif Foundation, reversing a decade of resistance. The precedent for coercive transfer is the June 2021 abduction in Bishkek of Sapat director **Orhan İnandı**, who resurfaced in Turkish custody. Turkey is Kyrgyzstan's principal non-Russian arms supplier (Bayraktar TB2, Akıncı, Aksungur, Anka), and Turkish plus OIC backing was decisive in the UN Security Council contest.

**Regional — the real success.** See §4. Borders settled with both Tajikistan and Uzbekistan; demarcation ~90% complete by July 2026, due to finish 2027; the former Uzbek exclaves Chongara and Tash-Tobo (~2,500 people) transferred to Kyrgyz jurisdiction on 23 June 2026 — though the government did not identify the Kyrgyz land transferred in return, publish a budget, or describe the legislative steps a border revision would normally require.

**Security.** The armed forces roughly doubled from ~11,000 (2018) to ~23,000, with a **$654m budget (~2% of GDP)** *(Official claim — General Staff figures, not independently verified)*. The State Department assesses that ~850 Kyrgyz citizens have joined terrorist groups since 2014, with 121 repatriated from northeast Syria in 2024 and 50+ fighters still detained there; it also warns that October 2024 amendments broadened the definition of "terrorist materials" in ways that could target political opponents.

## 9. Society and public opinion

### 9.1 The measurement blackout — a finding in its own right

**No independent national opinion poll on Kyrgyzstan has been published since January 2024.**

IRI's Center for Insights last released a Kyrgyzstan national survey for fieldwork of **21 December 2023 – 19 January 2024** (SIAR Research, face-to-face, n=1,500, MoE ±2.5, USAID-funded). Central Asia Barometer's public register stops at **Wave 14 (fieldwork 13 September – 15 October 2023, n=1,509)**. A sweep of IRI's resource index on 11 September 2026 found nothing later.

The two plausible causes are the **April 2024 "foreign representatives" law** and the **2025 termination of USAID**, which funded the IRI series. That is a substantive consequence of policy, not a research inconvenience: the country has lost the instrument by which its own citizens' views were measured, at exactly the moment the political system stopped offering other signals. Anyone — including this government — claiming to know what Kyrgyz people currently think is inferring, not measuring.

The ~88% approval figure for Japarov circulating via Bertelsmann has no traceable instrument behind it. **Treat every popularity claim about 2025–26 as unevidenced.**

### 9.2 The last real numbers (IRI, January 2024)

Worth recording, because they are the baseline against which everything since is guesswork — and because they are *not* uniformly flattering to the critics' case:

| | |
|---|---|
| Country direction | **82% right / 14% wrong** (up from 41% in Aug 2020) |
| Trust — Presidency | 71% positive |
| Trust — GKNB | 68% |
| Trust — Parliament | 48% (39% negative) |
| **Trust — courts** | **29% positive, 55% negative** |
| Most important problem | Cost of living 27%, unemployment 25%, **corruption 10%** |
| Perceived corrupt — courts / police / traffic police | 78% / 78% / 80% |
| Perceived corrupt — Office of the President | 45% |
| Politician trust (spontaneous) | **Japarov 37%, Tashiev 22%** |
| Satisfied with how democracy is developing | 66% |

Two honest readings of this. The government's: after three years, satisfaction was high, perceived corruption had fallen sharply from 2022 (courts 92% → 78%), and half of respondents said corruption had "no negative impact at all" on them, up from 28% in May 2023. The critics': the courts — the institution that has since delivered every political verdict in this report — were trusted by **29%** even at the peak of the government's popularity, and ordinary Kyrgyz ranked corruption third, behind prices and jobs.

Caveats: face-to-face interviewing at home, in a state where social-media posts draw multi-year sentences, maximises social-desirability pressure on political items. CAB concedes "some self-censorship may be present." Mode shifted from phone to in-person mid-series, breaking comparability. Economic items are more robust than political ones.

### 9.3 What can be evidenced without polling

**Household financial fragility is measurable and has worsened.** The National Bank's 2025 Financial Literacy Survey (M-Vector with the OSCE Programme Office, n=1,020, aged 18–79) found **50% could not last more than a month without their main income — up from 39% in 2021**. Eighteen percent could not last a week. Only **15%** have more than six months' reserves. Thirty-nine percent describe themselves as "barely making ends meet." This sits directly alongside 11.1% GDP growth.

**Living standards are improving in aggregate and unevenly in practice.** Real wages rose 15.3% in H1 2026. But inflation is ~11.5–11.7% and the **year-end forecast was revised up from 9% to 14–15%**. Housing and utilities inflation was 11.2%; lamb +23.1%, fresh fruit +16%, beef +14.2%. Petrol AI-92 reached 88.24 som/litre, up 6% month-on-month — with **Russia supplying ~95% of fuel**. The government has drafted a **$419m price-stabilisation plan for 2026–27**.

**Poverty: two lines, both worth citing.** On the **national** line (70,963 som/year, ~$810, under $68/month), poverty was **24% in 2025**, down from 25.7% — about 1.779 million people, with **child poverty at 29.7%**. On the World Bank's **$4.20/day 2021 PPP** line it was 9.1%. These are not contradictory; they measure different things, and anyone citing one without the other is choosing a number rather than reporting one.

Two details cut against the headline. **Extreme poverty rose, from 2.7% to 3.6%.** And **without income earned abroad, the 2025 poverty rate would have been 34.9% rather than 24%** — a third of the country is kept above the line by emigration, not by the domestic economy.

**Education is the starkest measure of state capacity.** In **PISA 2025** (published September 2026, 91 countries) Kyrgyzstan scored **mathematics 364 (79th), science 363 (80th), reading 344 (82nd)** against OECD averages of 463, 482 and 461 — a gap of roughly 100–120 points, several years of schooling. It is an improvement on placing last in all three domains in 2006, and it is still near the bottom. The 100+ new schools are buildings; this is what happens inside them.

**Emigration is both safety valve and exposure.** Estimates of the Kyrgyz stock in Russia span **300,000 to 854,000** depending on source and definition and could not be reconciled. Whatever the level, the direction is down. Russia's "controlled persons" register held **685,000 foreigners** by mid-2026. Since 2024 migrant children must pass Russian-language testing to enrol in school — **only 19% cleared documentation and testing in September 2025**. Kyrgyzstan's state **Tunguch** online school had 884 pupils studying from inside Russia by early 2026. Diversification is real but small: the UK seasonal-worker quota rose from ~1,500 (2022) to **10,000 (2025)**; South Korea's to just over 3,000. On **14 May 2026** the Cabinet approved the first-ever **Programme for the Reintegration of Returning Migrants 2026–2030**.

**Energy is the visible failure.** A national energy emergency has been in force since 1 August 2023, extended to 31 December 2026. Consumption was **19.1–19.3bn kWh in 2025 against domestic generation of 15.4bn**, requiring 3.9bn kWh of imports; for 2026 the gap widens to **~4.1bn kWh**. Toktogul supplies roughly 40% of electricity and its water level is critically low. The **peak-hour household cap was cut from 5 kW to 3 kW**; Bishkek dims at 10pm. Tariffs cover about **45% of production cost** ($0.018/kWh against $0.034), with annual increases scheduled to 2030 — which means the politically hardest part of this adjustment is still ahead.

**Religion and social conservatism.** A new Law on Freedom of Religion took effect **1 February 2025**: it bans face-covering religious dress in public (fine 20,000 som, ~$230), prohibits door-to-door proselytising, requires **muftiyat permission to teach religion**, and bars religious organisations from funding campaigns. The state muftiyat backed it, declaring the hijab obligatory but the niqab not — and Kyrgyzstan still permits hijab in schools and offices, unlike its neighbours. Enforcement in 2026 has mostly been police "preventive conversations." On 8–9 June 2026 the GKNB detained 31 alleged Katibat al-Tawhid wal-Jihad and Islamic State members in Osh and Batken.

**Gender.** Registered domestic violence rose **35% year-on-year to 10,164 cases in H1 2025**. The best prevalence estimate for bride kidnapping remains the 2015–16 nationally representative NSC survey with UN Women, UNFPA and IOM: **22.1% of marriages involved some form of abduction — 16.3% with consent, 5.8% without**. No 2025–26 incidence or prosecution data was located. Women's parliamentary representation rose to 30 of 90 — via quota.

**Attitudes to external powers** (CAB, latest waves to 2023, so pre-dating the blackout): **Turkey is the most favourably viewed** (84% favourable), ahead of Russia, Iran, China and the US. On China, general favourability is around 60–70% but specific anxieties are far sharper: **~80% worried about rising debt to China and over 90% concerned about Chinese land purchases** — the regional peak. On Russia's war, 33–35% called it justified to some degree, with those calling it unjustified falling from 36% to 28%; consumption of Russian media is the strongest single correlate (40% approval among consumers vs 25% among non-consumers). Note that the two topics on which Kyrgyz citizens are most anxious — **Chinese land and foreign investment in land** — are precisely the two on which people were prosecuted in 2026 (Askarbekov, Bayamanov).

### 9.4 Disengagement, and what protest is left

**Political disengagement is the dominant fact.** Turnout of 34–36% nationally and **26–29% in Bishkek**, against a backdrop of banned outlets and jailed opposition figures, is the clearest available measure of public mood. OSW's judgement that *"Kyrgyz society has largely lost interest in politics"* is, so far, borne out: no mobilisation followed the Tashiev affair.

**Protest that survives is narrow and material.** Right-hand-drive vehicle operators held permitted rallies in April and July 2026 against taxi licensing. Land allocation to foreign investors triggered the summer's main confrontation. Environmental protest survives in petition form — a campaign against F1H2O powerboat racing on Lake Issyk-Kul, which officials dismissed as "amateurish." **Crowd sizes are not reported in any source**, which is itself indicative.

## 10. Assessment

### What is genuinely good

1. **Peace on two borders.** The Treaty of Khujand is the single most consequential achievement of this government and possibly of any Kyrgyz government since independence. Hundreds of people died in 2021–22. Nobody has died since. It required land swaps that were politically expensive, and it has held.
2. **Fiscal capacity.** A state that could not tax now can. The customs number — 28.9bn to 157.4bn som with the same staff — is the most honest single indicator, and hostile assessments credit it.
3. **Poverty reduction is real** on the international line, whatever measure you prefer, and pensions and public-sector wages have risen substantially.
4. **Digitalisation has reduced petty corruption**, which is the form of corruption most citizens actually encounter.
5. **Infrastructure that was rhetorical is now physical** — Kambarata-1 financed, the CKU railway under construction.
6. **Organised crime was confronted**, and BTI's 7/10 on monopoly of force reflects a genuine change.

### What is genuinely bad

1. **There is no longer any lawful way to hold power accountable.** No independent judiciary (Freedom House: 0/4), no opposition party, no parliamentary check (87–0, no questions), no independent domestic election observation (two organisations, two observers), and no investigative media inside the country.
2. **The criminal law is now the primary instrument of politics.** "Calling for mass unrest," "inciting discord," and "extremism" have been applied to a Facebook share, a satire channel, a musician, and criticism of Chinese investment.
3. **Selective justice is demonstrable, not merely alleged.** Five years for cameramen; probation and no confiscation for a convicted coup plotter.
4. **Anti-corruption without law is asset redistribution.** ~$800m extracted in 2025 alone with no court, no methodology and no audit, while OCCRP traces specific assets to regime-connected buyers.
5. **Property rights have been destroyed for anyone large enough to be worth taking.** BTI's finding that by late 2024 no medium or large business was safe is the clearest explanation of why FDI sits at 3% of GDP during a boom.
6. **The torture-prevention mechanism was abolished** in the same year that credible torture allegations were made against the security service.
7. **Human capital is not improving at the rate the buildings suggest.** PISA 2025 puts Kyrgyzstan 79th–82nd of 91 countries, 100+ points below the OECD average. A hundred new schools is a real achievement; it is not the same achievement as an education system.
8. **The country lost the ability to measure itself.** The foreign-representatives law and the end of USAID funding ended independent polling and independent election observation in the same two-year window.

### What is genuinely contested or unresolved

- **How much of the growth is real.** The 27.4%-of-GDP "Errors and Omissions" figure means a quarter of measured economic activity is inferred, not observed. A four-point forecast spread for 2026 reflects that.
- **Whether the Tashiev case was a purge or a settlement.** The evidence is genuinely ambiguous (§2.2).
- **The gold export gap.** ~52 tonnes unaccounted; the EAEU monetary-gold explanation is plausible and untested.
- **Japarov's actual popularity.** Probably high — rural, populist, resource-nationalist, and delivering visible goods. Probably not 88%. Unmeasurable under current conditions.
- **Whether the state prosecutes its own or only its rivals.** The Kyrgyzneftegaz case and the arrest of ~100 tax officials in December 2024 cut against a pure kleptocracy model. But the state used Temirov Live's 2022 reporting to do it, having jailed the reporters.

### Risks and scenarios to watch

| Risk | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **Secondary sanctions on Kyrgyz banks** | **Highest-probability material shock.** Named explicitly by the World Bank. The EU has already crossed the threshold once; the garment sector shows what the end of arbitrage looks like |
| **Remittance shock** | Moderate-high. Migrant stock down 40%; Russia's 2027 employer-tying scheme is the trigger to watch |
| **Energy crisis** | Ongoing, not prospective. Emergency in force to end-2026; cascade blackout August 2026 |
| **Southern elite alienation** | Structural and unhedged since February 2026. No mechanism now exists to surface it before it becomes disorder |
| **Fiscal deterioration** | Already forecast: surplus 2.6% → deficit 3.5%, partly caused by the wage rises that buy consent |
| **January 2027 election** | Low risk of surprise; the question is legitimacy, not outcome |
| **Succession** | Unhedged. The designated successor has been convicted and barred. There is no plan B in public view |

**The central structural judgement:** Kyrgyzstan's three previous changes of government happened because pressure had somewhere to go — streets, parliament, parties, press. Each of those channels has now been closed. That does not make an explosion likely in the near term; disengagement is real and the security services are stronger. It does mean that *if* pressure builds — an energy winter, a sanctions shock, a remittance collapse — there is no longer any low-cost way for it to be expressed. The researcher's line worth keeping is Aigerim Turgunbaeva's: *"If repression continues and the economy falters… the next explosion may be uncontrollable."*

## 11. Contradictions, corrections and gaps

**Corrections made during research** (recorded because they affect other reporting):

- Wikipedia's "2026 in Kyrgyzstan" states Tashiev and seven others were **arrested** on 12 May 2026. This contradicts RFE/RL, The Diplomat and 24.kg, which have the three principal defendants free on bail throughout. **Treated as unreliable.**
- The Tashiev verdict was **2 July 2026**, not 3 July (OCCRP and several wires used their publication date).
- Matraimov was extradited in **March 2024**, not 2025.
- CACI's report that Tashiev faced **"high treason"** charges appears unsupported; the charges were Articles 326 and 337.
- CIVICUS attributes the 17 February term ruling to the **Supreme** Court; RFE/RL, 24.kg and ODIHR all attribute it to the **Constitutional** Court. The latter is correct.

**Unresolved source conflicts:**

- **Turnout, Nov 2025**: ODIHR reports CEC preliminary 34.31%; other sources report 36.40% final. Not reconciled from a primary CEC source.
- **Candidates registered**: ODIHR 465; Norwegian Helsinki Committee 476.
- **Self-dissolution date**: ODIHR and OSW 25 September 2025; Wikipedia 24 September.
- **2025 inflation**: World Bank 8.2% (average); IMF-derived reporting 9.4%. Probably average vs end-period, not confirmed.
- **Tashiev confiscation**: Times of Central Asia reported assets confiscated in July; Kloop reported on 10 September that confiscation was not applied under the new statute. The appellate ruling appears to have removed it.
- **Public debt**: World Bank 39.5% of GDP (end-2025); IMF WEO 36.0%. A 3.5-point gap.
- **2026 fiscal outturn**: World Bank forecast a 3.5%-of-GDP deficit; reported January–May data show revenues up 44.1% and a surplus. The forecast appears to be failing.
- **RSF 2025 rank**: The Diplomat cites 122nd; CIVICUS and RSF's own index data give 144th. The latter is used here.
- **Migrant stock in Russia**: estimates span 300,000 (RFE/RL) to 854,000 (2022 census-based), with 502,242 from the Kyrgyz Labour Ministry and ~350,000 from the National Bank. Irreconcilable; treat any single figure with suspicion.
- **Remittances as % of GDP**: sources give 15%, 17.6%, 19.9% and 24% for overlapping periods. The World Bank's 19.9% for 2025 is used here.
- **Japarov's succession interview**: dated 13 August by most sources, 17 August by one.
- **Exports 2025**: World Bank shows goods-and-services exports down 16.4% in real terms; IMF-derived reporting cites a 44.5% fall. Different measures; not reconciled.

**What could not be established:**

- **Why the 10 September reclassification happened, or who decided it.** Proceedings were closed; The Diplomat states outright it cannot be explained.
- **Whether the Art. 195 conviction disqualifies Tashiev** from the January 2027 ballot under the 2025 criminal-record bar.
- **Any current independent polling** on presidential approval or institutional trust — nothing since January 2024, and nothing at all on the Tashiev affair, the niqab ban, the land legislation or the 2027 election.
- **Health-system data**: out-of-pocket share of spending, physician density, maternal mortality, or medical emigration. WHO country pages did not return figures.
- **Any skilled-emigration ("brain drain") data.**
- **A 2025 or 2026 Gini coefficient** — latest located is 26.4 for 2022.
- **Domestic violence figures beyond H1 2025**, or any protection-order and conviction data.
- **Post-2010 ethnic Uzbek representation** in parliament, civil service, police or courts, and current Uzbek-language school numbers.
- **Protest crowd sizes** in 2025–26 — not reported in any source consulted.
- **Whether the NBKR changed its policy rate at any point in 2026** — the last documented move is to 11% in November 2025.
- **Any 2026 sovereign rating action.** S&P assigned B+/B stable (28 March 2025), Fitch B stable (24 April 2025), Moody's moved B3 to positive outlook (11 July 2025); officials requested an upgrade in April 2026 and none was found.
- **Whether the EU has opened a formal GSP+ review** tied to the foreign-representatives law.
- **Whether Kyrgyzstan appears on a signed 2025/26 US travel-ban proclamation** (as distinct from the visa freeze and bond requirement, which are confirmed).
- **Whether the anti-trans bill passed second and third readings** after 4 June 2026.
- **Whether the TikTok block** (in force since 18 April 2024) has been lifted following a February 2026 ministry proposal.
- **Whether kusturization receipts are audited at all.** No audit of the 70.68bn som (2025) or the cumulative total was located anywhere.
- **The names of the 50 companies** deregistered in May 2026 or the 19 in August — withheld by the Ministry of Justice.
- **Azattyk's current registration status** under the 2025 media law.
- **A full current cabinet list from an official source** — gov.kg and president.kg were unreachable.
