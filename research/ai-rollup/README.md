# AI Roll-Ups of SMBs — The Good, The Bad, The Ugly

*Research memo. August 2026. Written to be argued with, not agreed with.*

> **Part I** (below) — the verticals: the screen, the scoreboard, deep dives, traps.
> **[Part II](#part-ii--where-to-do-it-us-vs-europe-and-what-i-would-actually-do)** — the decision: US vs Europe, both financing paths, and my actual call.

---

## 0. Read this part even if you read nothing else

Four things I need you to internalise before we talk about any specific industry.

**1. You are not buying a business. You are buying a job with debt attached.**
A roll-up is not an investment thesis, it's an operating grind. The AI is maybe 10% of the work. The other 90% is: a 61-year-old owner who won't return your calls, a service manager who quits three weeks after close, a customer concentration problem nobody disclosed, and payroll on Friday. Everyone pitching AI roll-ups on X has never had to make payroll on Friday. Decide right now whether you actually want that life for eight years. If not, stop here — you'll save yourself $500k.

**2. The "AI" is not the edge. The *deal flow* is the edge.**
Every buyer in every one of these verticals has access to the same models you do. GPT/Claude/Gemini are a commodity input, priced identically for you and for the $10B platform. What is *not* commoditised: getting an off-market owner to sell you their business at 4x instead of a broker running it to 7x. The entire return sits in the entry price and in retention post-close. AI is a margin lever, not a moat. Anyone telling you otherwise is selling you a fund.

**3. The bear case is real and it is well-argued.**
The strongest criticisms — and they hold up:
- Cost advantage from AI is *ephemeral*. Once your competitor adopts it, you both cut price and the savings flow to the customer, not to you. In verticals where the customer can shop on price, AI destroys the industry's profit pool rather than transferring it to you. ([equal.vc](https://newsletter.equal.vc/p/why-most-ai-enabled-roll-ups-will))
- Multiple arbitrage (buy at 5x, sell at 10x) rarely materialises, because you bid *against* other people who also know the trick, and entry prices inflate. ([Dave Friedman](https://davefriedman.substack.com/p/ai-rollups-wont-work))
- Integration debt is worse than modelled. Every acquired firm has its own systems, contracts, compliance quirks. Harmonising them takes far longer than the model assumes.
- MIT's *GenAI Divide* study: **95% of enterprise GenAI pilots delivered zero measurable P&L impact.** Not 50%. 95%. ([MIT via Fortune/Yahoo](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/mit-report-95-generative-ai-105412686.html))
- Making a services business more efficient does not make it a software business. Efficiency ≠ business-model transformation. ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/06/27/ai-rollup-investment-strategy/))

**4. But the bull case has receipts now, which it didn't 18 months ago.**
- **Current** (ex-Crete Professionals Alliance, Thrive-backed): 30+ accounting firms, $300M+ revenue. Their tax AI did **7,000 returns in its first season, ~97% draft accuracy, ~⅓ practitioner time saved, ~50% throughput lift.** ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260602293257/en/Crete-Professionals-Alliance-Rebrands-as-Current-to-Equip-Independent-Accounting-Firms-to-Compete-at-Enterprise-Scale))
- **Long Lake** (General Catalyst, HOA management): **$100M EBITDA in under two years**, then bought Amex GBT for $6.3B in May 2026. ([PitchBook](https://pitchbook.com/news/articles/general-catalysts-6-3b-amex-deal-puts-its-ai-roll-up-strategy-on-display))
- **Crescendo** (call centres): reportedly ~4x the margins of a traditional contact centre.
- General Catalyst studied **70 service categories** and picked **10 where 30–70% of tasks are automatable with today's models**, targeting margin transformation from **5–15% → 35%+**. $1.5B committed. Thrive committed $1B with OpenAI taking a stake. ([Capital Founders](https://www.capitalfounders.io/playbooks/ai-enabled-roll-ups/))

So: the strategy works. It has worked for people with $1.5B, a bench of ex-McKinsey operators, and a proprietary model-training partnership with OpenAI. The question this memo answers is *which version of it works for you*.

## 1. The screen — seven filters that decide everything

Before any vertical: run it through these. A vertical must pass **at least 6 of 7**. Most fail on #5 and #7, which is exactly why most AI roll-ups will fail.

| # | Filter | Why it decides the outcome |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Fragmented** — thousands of sub-$5M owners, no dominant player | Otherwise you have no runway and you're bidding against strategics on every deal |
| 2 | **Labour is the dominant cost**, and it's *expensive* labour | AI replaces labour cost. Replacing a $12/hr offshore worker with inference doesn't pay. Replacing a $95k licensed professional does. |
| 3 | **Recurring / contracted revenue**, 60%+ | Project revenue has no terminal value and you re-buy your customer every year |
| 4 | **Aging owners with no succession** | This is your source of cheap entry multiples. It's the whole silver-tsunami trade. |
| 5 | **The customer cannot easily shop on price** — sticky, switching costs, or regulated | ⚠️ **The one everybody skips.** If customers can shop, AI savings get competed away to zero and you've just funded a price war. |
| 6 | **Revenue-side synergy exists at scale**, not just cost cuts | Cost advantage is temporary. Better pricing/access/cross-sell at scale is durable. |
| 7 | **AI improves the unit economics without creating catastrophic liability** | 95% model accuracy is a miracle in marketing and a lawsuit in tax, medicine, or law |

**Filter 5 is the killer.** It's the difference between insurance brokerage (customer never shops, premium rises with inflation, your commission is a % of a rising number) and translation services (price fell from ~$0.20/word to ~$0.002/word — the entire industry's profit pool evaporated in 24 months).

Ask about every vertical: *when AI makes this 60% cheaper to deliver, who captures the 60% — me, or my customer?*

## 3. Deep dives

### 3.1 Accounting & tax — *the most obvious trade, and therefore the most dangerous*

**The business.** 46,000–52,000 CPA firms; 120,000+ firms across accounting/tax/bookkeeping/payroll; average establishment under 10 people. ~$157–250B of US revenue depending on definition. Partners are old, CPA candidate numbers fell ~30% between 2016 and 2021, and there is no natural buyer for a $1.5M-revenue practice in Ohio.

**The good.**
- The single best labour-arbitrage target that exists. Expensive licensed labour doing enormous volumes of structured document work — exactly what filter 2 asks for.
- Entry multiples for the small end are *absurd* relative to what the cash flow is worth: **1.0–1.3x revenue, ~2.75x SDE median** for sub-$2–3M practices. Compare to 5–8x EBITDA the moment you're above $3M revenue. That gap **is** the trade — you're being paid to aggregate.
- Revenue is genuinely sticky. Clients don't switch accountants; they inherit them and die with them.
- Proof it works: Current's tax AI, 7,000 returns, 97% draft accuracy, 50% throughput lift, ⅓ time saved. That is a real, measured, published number in a real production season.

**The bad.**
- **It is the single most crowded trade in America.** ~900 roll-up transactions in the profession in 2025 alone; half the top 30 firms have taken PE money. You are bidding against Thrive's $1B and every regional consolidator simultaneously.
- Regulatory structure is a tax on the deal: CPA ownership rules force an alternative practice structure (attest entity stays CPA-owned, MSO takes the non-attest business). More lawyers, more cost, more ways to be wrong.
- Talent: 84% of accountant turnover is voluntary. Your asset walks out the door every night and can start a competing practice on Monday with the client list in their head.

**The ugly.**
- **Realisation compression is coming for you too.** If AI cuts prep time 60–80%, and every firm has AI, why does the client keep paying $1,800 for a return? The answer had better be "because they never think about it" — and for the compliance base that's mostly true today. But billable-hour → fixed-fee → AI-priced is a one-way ratchet and you're buying at today's revenue.
- The exit is not guaranteed. Xeinadin — a PE-backed accounting roll-up — has struggled to find a buyer at its ~£1B ask. The greater-fool exit is exactly what breaks first when rates and sentiment turn.
- **Cuban's question:** *"So you're going to outbid Josh Kushner for accounting firms? With what?"*

**What I'd actually do.** Do NOT buy $3M+ firms — that's PE's turf and PE's price. Buy **$400k–$1.2M revenue bookkeeping/CAS practices** (not audit, not complex tax) in secondary markets, off-market, at 0.8–1.0x revenue with heavy seller notes. Bookkeeping is more automatable than tax, less regulated, more recurring, and PE won't get out of bed for it. Consolidate 8–12 of them into one back office. You're not competing with Current; you're operating one tier below where they can economically reach.

**Kill criteria.** If entry multiples on sub-$1M practices go above 1.3x revenue in your market, stop buying. If your realised revenue per retained client declines two years running, the pricing ratchet has arrived and the thesis is dead.

### 3.2 Insurance agencies — *the best business on this list, at the worst price*

**The business.** Independent P&C agencies. You own a commission stream against a carrier panel, typically 10–25% of premium.

**The good.** This is the only vertical on the list that passes **all seven filters**, and it's the one the smartest bear-case writer picked as his exception.
- Customers never shop. Retention above 90% is normal.
- **Premiums rise with inflation → your revenue rises automatically while your cost to service falls.** Your ACV is inflation-protected. Nothing else here has this.
- Genuine revenue-side synergy: larger premium volume → **higher commission rates from carriers** + contingents + cross-sell. This satisfies filter 6, which almost nothing else does.
- Retention >90%, commercial-lines mix >60%, and 8+ standard-market carrier appointments are the explicit multiple drivers — all controllable.

**The bad.** The price. **7–13x EBITDA; $1M+ EBITDA agencies averaged 11.8x in H1 2025.** Acrisure, HUB, Brown & Brown, AssuredPartners, Higginbotham, Patriot, Alera and dozens of regionals are all bidding. Tuck-ins clear 7.0–8.5x. There is no cheap corner of this market that isn't cheap for a reason.

**The ugly — the stuff that kills first-time buyers.**
- **Carrier appointments do not automatically transfer.** You can close on an agency and discover the carriers that write 40% of the book won't appoint you. Verify appointment transferability in writing, per carrier, before LOI. This alone has killed deals post-close.
- **Producers may own their expirations.** In many agencies the producer, not the agency, owns the book. You can buy a "book of business" and find the book has legs.
- Contingent commissions swing with loss ratios — one bad cat year and a chunk of your EBITDA is gone through no fault of your own.
- E&O tail exposure. Deals with real E&O history run 180+ days.

**What I'd actually do.** You cannot win a competitive process here — you'll pay 9x. The only way in is **off-market, relationship-sourced, from a retiring owner who cares who takes his clients**, with a 3-year earn-out on retention. Target commercial lines in a niche (contractors, trucking, restaurants, ag) where you can build actual underwriting knowledge. AI's role is small and unglamorous: certificates, endorsements, renewal prep, submission packaging, claims intake. It's a 20–30% cost lever, not a 70% one — which is fine, because the *business* is the point, not the AI.

**Kill criteria.** If you can't get carrier appointments confirmed in writing pre-close, walk. Every time.

### 3.3 MSPs / IT services — *the best fit for who you actually are*

**The business.** 150,000–200,000 MSPs globally; only 5,000–10,000 meet recognised maturity standards. 466 North American deals in 2025 (+20% YoY), PE involved in 72%.

**The good.**
- Contracted MRR, multi-year agreements, brutal switching costs (they hold the keys to the client's entire infrastructure). Filter 5: **passes**. Nobody rips out their MSP to save 8%.
- Enormous quality dispersion → sub-$5M MSPs trade at **~4x**, scaled/AI-enabled/security-heavy platforms at **10–14x**. That's a 3-turn arbitrage on operational improvement alone, before AI.
- The AI leverage is real and near-term: L1 ticket deflection, automated triage, documentation, patch/monitoring summarisation, QBR generation. This is the exact workload frontier models are best at.
- **Cybersecurity attach is a 55–70% gross margin add-on** you can cross-sell into every acquired book. That's filter 6 — a revenue-side synergy, not just a cost cut.
- **This is the one vertical where an AI consultancy has a structural, non-fake advantage.** You already speak the language, you can diligence the tech stack yourself, and you can sell your own AI services *into* the acquired client base.

**The bad.**
- Margin compression is the industry's defining complaint in 2026: clients demand more endpoints, more compliance, more AI — and won't pay more. Microsoft CSP margins are under pressure.
- Techs are scarce and mobile. Commodity break-fix and project revenue are getting crushed while security and specialisation get premiums.

**The ugly.**
- **You are buying unlimited cyber liability.** Ransomware was in 44% of confirmed breaches in 2025. One ransomware event through your RMM tool and you own the incident for every client simultaneously. 91.7% of MSPs carry cyber insurance and it is not enough. Diligence the *contracts*: limitation of liability, mandatory client-side cyber insurance, and the security posture of the acquired shop's own tooling. An MSP that hasn't hardened its own RMM is an unexploded bomb, not an acquisition.
- Many small MSPs are one senior tech deep. Buy the business, lose the tech, lose the clients.

**What I'd actually do.** Buy 3–5 sub-$5M MSPs in one metro at 4–5x. Consolidate onto one PSA/RMM stack (this is the real work and it takes a year). Deploy AI on L1 deflection + documentation. Attach security to every seat. Then you're a $15–20M revenue, security-forward, AI-enabled regional platform trading at 10–13x. **That is the cleanest 3-turn multiple arbitrage on this entire page** and it doesn't require the AI to be magic — it just requires you to not lose clients while integrating.

**Kill criteria.** If the target's contracts don't cap liability, or there's no client-side cyber insurance requirement, walk. If >30% of revenue is project/break-fix, it's not an MSP, it's a computer shop.

### 3.4 Medical billing / RCM — *the highest AI leverage anywhere, and the widest multiple gap*

**The business.** Outsourced billing and collections for physician practices, surgical groups, DME, behavioural health, etc.

**The good.**
- **The multiple gap is the most extreme on this list.** Owner-operated sub-$1M-EBITDA billing shops trade at **2.5–4.0x**. Mid-size at 3.5–6x. Scaled platforms at 6–11x. And AI/automation-enabled platforms are being valued at **12–25x+**. If you believe half of that spread, this is the trade.
- Labour is nearly the entire cost base, it's structured document work, and it's exactly what models do well: coding, claim scrubbing, denial classification, appeal letter drafting, eligibility checks, payment posting.
- Revenue is a % of collections — recurring, contracted, and it *rises* when you perform better. Rare alignment.
- Sector is consolidating hard (R1 at $8.9B, Waystar/Iodine $1.25B, GeBBS at 17.2x, EQT/KKR/Vista/Silver Lake all active) — meaning a real exit path exists at scale.

**The bad.**
- Customer concentration is endemic in small billing shops. Losing one 300-provider group can be 40% of revenue.
- Clients *can* shop — billing is priced as a % of collections and it's a comparable number. Filter 5 is **partially failed**. Expect the 4–7% of collections fee to drift down as AI spreads. Your defence is performance (higher net collection rate), not price.
- Offshore is the incumbent competitor, not the status quo. You're not replacing $95k coders, you're often replacing $6/hr Manila coders — and that's the exact case where the bears are right that inference doesn't beat wages by enough.

**The ugly.**
- **95% accuracy is a compliance event.** False claims, upcoding, and HIPAA are not areas where "the model hallucinated" is a defence. You need human-in-the-loop on anything touching a claim, which caps your automation ceiling well below the marketing numbers. Payer audits and clawbacks land on *you*.
- Payer rule changes are constant and unannounced; your automation is permanently half-broken by definition.

**What I'd actually do.** Pick **one specialty** (surgical, ortho, behavioural health, DME) and go deep — specialty-specific coding knowledge is the only defensible asset here. Buy 2–3 shops at 3–4x. Measure yourself on net collection rate and days-in-AR, not headcount saved. Sell the *outcome* (we collect 4% more of what you're owed) rather than the *price*, which is the only way to escape the fee ratchet.

**Kill criteria.** Any target with >25% revenue in one client. Any target that can't produce clean NCR/DAR history per client.

### 3.5 Property management (HOA & commercial) — *the model with the most public proof*

**The business.** Third-party management of HOAs, multifamily, commercial, or SFR portfolios, on recurring per-door/per-association management fees.

**The good.**
- **Long Lake is the single most convincing proof point of the entire AI roll-up thesis** — HOA management, $100M EBITDA in under two years. It works, at scale, in this vertical, demonstrably.
- HOA/CAM and multifamily platforms trade at **9–13x** while sub-scale SFR/mixed books with 500+ doors go for **4.5–5.5x**. Big, harvestable spread.
- The AI surface is broad and boring, which is where AI actually works: leasing enquiries, resident comms, maintenance triage and vendor dispatch, board packets, meeting minutes, violation letters, arrears chasing. Reported results: 37–42% faster work-order completion, ~80% cut in manual coordination.
- Ancillary revenue (leasing fees, maintenance markup, inspections) lifts revenue-per-door — filter 6 satisfied.
- Sticky: switching managers is painful and requires a board vote.

**The bad.**
- Contracts are typically annual and cancellable. Doors churn. The "recurring" revenue is recurring until a board gets annoyed.
- It's a low-margin, high-complaint business. Residents and boards are exhausting in a way spreadsheets don't capture.

**The ugly.**
- **Trust accounting.** You hold other people's money. Commingling is a licence-revocation and criminal-referral event in Texas and Florida. Signatory controls, monthly reconciliation, fidelity bonds, five years of records. If the shop you're buying has one person who both enters invoices and signs cheques, assume fraud until proven otherwise, because that is the standard fact pattern.
- Brokerage licensing: managing property without the required licence can be a Class A misdemeanour and you may have to **disgorge fees already collected**. Verify licensure per state before, not after.

**What I'd actually do.** HOA/CAM specifically, not SFR. Boards are institutional buyers with long memories and long contracts; individual landlords are not. Buy in one metro for density (site visits, vendor networks, and inspectors don't scale across states). Get a forensic look at the trust accounts on day one of diligence — earlier than the QoE.

### 3.6 Fire & life safety / mandated compliance services — *the quietest good idea here*

**The business.** Sprinkler, alarm, extinguisher, backflow, elevator ITM (inspection, testing, maintenance).

**The good.** This is the closest thing to a legally guaranteed revenue stream in the SMB world. **NFPA 25 and NFPA 72 legally require periodic inspection and testing of every installed system.** Your customer does not buy this because they want it; they buy it because the fire marshal makes them. Filter 5 isn't just passed, it's *legislated*. Every install becomes an annuity. Route density economics like pest control, but non-discretionary.

**The bad.** The buy side already knows: Pye-Barker (Altas + Leonard Green), Summit (BDT & MSD), AI Fire/Impact (Blackstone), Marmic (KKR), Sciens (Carlyle). Very heavy competition for anything of scale.

**The ugly.** Licensed technicians are the constraint and they're scarce; AI cannot climb a ladder. The AI leverage here is real but confined to scheduling/routing, deficiency-report generation, quote-from-inspection conversion, and compliance-deadline chasing — call it a 15–25% SG&A lever, not a transformation.

**What I'd actually do.** Only if you find an off-market sub-$1.5M-EBITDA operator in a metro none of the platforms have entered. The AI angle: automatically convert every inspection deficiency into a priced repair quote within 24 hours. Most small operators leave 30–50% of deficiency repair revenue on the table because nobody follows up. That's not a cost cut — that's revenue synergy from the same field visit, and it's the highest-ROI AI application on this entire page.

### 3.7 The ones people will pitch you that you should refuse

**Marketing agencies.** The pitch is seductive: high labour cost, fragmented, AI eats the work. It's a trap. Your clients have the same tools and are already using them to cut your fee — 39% of CMOs are cutting agency budgets, agency headcount fell 8% in 2025, Forrester projects -15% of the US ad workforce by end-2027. Distressed agencies trade at 0.7–1.1x revenue. **When both sides of the table get the same tool, the side with the money wins.** Fails filter 5 more completely than anything else here.

**Translation, transcription, court reporting, basic BPO.** These aren't roll-up targets, they're the *casualties*. Translation went ~$0.20/word → ~$0.002/word. Legal transcription went $3–5/min → $0.15–0.25/min. Buying these is buying the buggy-whip factory in 1908 because the whips are still selling.

**Anything the government prices.** ABA therapy (NY rate -14.2% in one cut; CARD bankrupted under Blackstone), Medicaid-heavy home care, independent pharmacy (losing money on 60%+ of Part D scripts). You can be the best operator in the world and a state budget committee can delete your margin on a Tuesday.

**Vet and dental.** You'd be buying into someone else's ZIRP hangover. ~$45B went into vet 2017–2022; Thrive Pet has already done a distressed debt exchange. Wait for the workout, don't join the pile.

## 4. The structural traps — these kill you regardless of vertical

1. **You will overpay for the platform and it will define your returns.** The first deal sets your cost of capital and your credibility. Pay 4x for something boring rather than 7x for something exciting.
2. **Integration is the whole job and nobody budgets for it.** Different systems, contracts, comp plans, and cultures. Assume 12–18 months per acquisition to actually be on one stack, not the 90 days in the model.
3. **Key-person risk is the real asset risk.** In every white-collar vertical here, the asset walks out at 6pm. Structure retention: earn-outs on retained revenue, rollover equity, 3-year employment with real economics. **60–70% cash at close / ~30% rollover** is the emerging AI-roll-up standard for a reason — it keeps the founder in the boat.
4. **AI savings get competed away unless you're protected by filter 5.** Model this explicitly: assume your price falls 20% over 5 years and see if the deal still works. If it doesn't, you're betting on a temporary edge.
5. **Debt is not free anymore.** Total leverage caps have come in to 4–6x from 6–7x; unitranche prices at SOFR+500–700. The 2021 math does not run in 2026.
6. **The exit is an assumption, not a fact.** Xeinadin at ~£1B is still looking. Underwrite to cash flow you'd be happy holding forever, and treat the multiple expansion as an option, not the plan.
7. **95% of AI pilots produce no P&L impact** — and the ones that work are 3x more likely to be bought than built (67% success blending external expertise vs 22% IT-only). Don't build a model. Buy tools and build *workflow*.

## 5. The financing reality — and one thing I need to check with you

### ⚠️ The SBA door may be closed to you

This is the single most consequential fact in this memo and I want to flag it before you spend a month on anything else.

As of **March 1, 2026**, SBA policy (implementing EO 14159) restricts 7(a), 504, Microloan and Surety Bond Guarantee programs to businesses owned by **US citizens or US nationals whose principal residence is in the United States**. Reporting on the change indicates **any** non-citizen ownership — including green-card holders, and reportedly as little as 1% — makes the business ineligible. ([SBA policy notice](https://www.sba.gov/document/policy-notice-5000-865754-policy-updates-comply-executive-order-14159-regarding-citizenship-requirements-obtaining-7a-504), [BRG](https://www.thinkbrg.com/insights/publications/sba-citizenship-eligibility-changes-implications-for-lenders-and-operational-considerations/), [Malescu Law](https://malesculaw.com/green-card-holders-barred-from-sba-loans-starting-march-1-2026/))

**Why this matters so much:** the entire self-funded SMB acquisition playbook runs on SBA 7(a) — 90% financing, 10-year amortisation, 10% equity injection of which half can be a full-standby seller note. Nothing else in the market gives a first-time buyer that leverage. Remove it and your required equity goes from ~5–10% to **25–40%**.

**If you are not a US citizen resident in the US, then:**
- The US sub-$5M market becomes roughly 4–8x harder to enter, and

## 9. "Why does citizenship matter?" — because people solve the wrong half of the problem

There are two separate locks on the door, and almost everyone confuses them.

**Lock 1 — the right to be there and run it.** That's a visa problem. It's solvable.
**Lock 2 — the right to cheap leverage.** That's the SBA. It is *not* solvable, and a visa does nothing for it.

### Why the SBA is not a "nice to have"

SBA 7(a) is the largest subsidy in small-business M&A anywhere on earth. **90% financing, 10-year amortisation, ~Prime + 2.75% (7.75–11.5%), and a 10% equity injection of which half can be a seller note on full standby.** No commercial lender on the planet offers a first-time buyer 90% LTV against goodwill with no hard asset security. The entire US self-funded searcher ecosystem — every podcast, every "I bought a $2M EBITDA business with $150k" story — is *built on that one government guarantee.*

Since **1 March 2026**, it's restricted to US citizens and US nationals whose principal residence is in the US. Reporting indicates **any** non-citizen ownership, even 1%, disqualifies the business — green-card holders included.

### The trap: a visa doesn't restore it

This is the part that costs people a year of their life.

- **E-2 treaty investor** — if you hold a treaty-country passport (Kazakhstan appears on published 2026 treaty lists; Russia does not — verify on travel.state.gov, that list moves), you can invest in and run a US business. **You still cannot get an SBA loan.**
- **L-1A intracompany transferee** — genuinely interesting for you specifically, because you already own an operating foreign consultancy. You transfer yourself as an executive to a US affiliate of Kenius. Unlike E-2, **it requires no personal capital investment**, it's faster and cheaper than EB-5, and it has a clean path to a green card via **EB-1C with no PERM**. Note USCIS may not treat a *purchased* business as a "new office," which changes the initial approval period — get that structured by counsel before you sign anything.
- **Green card via EB-1C** — and you *still* can't get an SBA loan, because the rule is citizenship, not residency.

So the honest arithmetic: **even on the fastest immigration path, SBA eligibility is naturalisation-distance away — call it 6–8 years.** Plan as if it does not exist. It doesn't, for you, for this decade.

### What that actually does to the maths

Same business, $5M enterprise value, 5x on $1M EBITDA:

| | US citizen | You (non-citizen) |
|---|---|---|
| Senior debt | 90% SBA | 50–60% (conventional / private credit, 2–4x EBITDA) |
| Seller note | ~5% (standby, counts as equity) | 10–25% |
| **Your cash** | **~$250–500k** | **$1.25–2.0M** |
| Rate | ~Prime + 2.75% | SOFR + 500–700bps |
| Leverage cap | ~6x+ effective | 4–6x total, and lenders are strict on EBITDA quality |

**That's a 4x difference in cash required for the identical asset.** That is the entire reason I asked, and it is the single most important number in this memo.
