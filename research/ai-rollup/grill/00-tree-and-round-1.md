## Settled by research (no need to ask)

| # | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **A3.4** | ✅ **Confirmed with a caveat that makes it worse.** A non-licensed person can own an electrical contractor in most states — but must designate a qualifying master electrician. In **Colorado** the firm must be owned wholly/partly by a master electrician *or* employ one supervisory. In **Minnesota** the responsible individual must be an owner, officer or member — or a W-2 employee actively engaged and not employed by any other contractor. **So in several states you must give equity or a board seat to a licensed electrician to hold the licence.** The single point of failure is worse than I described. |
| **A3.3** | ✅ **Confirmed, and it's the most concentrated bet in the economy.** No broad pullback as of mid-2026 — Alphabet raised 2026 capex to $195–205B, Amazon ~$220B, Meta $130–145B. Vacancy ~1.4% vs 20%+ in 2001; contracts precede construction. **But** hyperscaler capex-to-revenue at 31–83% is flagged as "utility-level" and the explicit watch signal. |
| **A1.1** | ⚠️ **Modify.** HBR: **more than two-thirds of roll-ups fail to create any value for investors.** McKinsey counters that many small deals beat big-bang M&A. NBER on anaesthesia roll-ups found prices rose **25–35%** after add-ons — i.e. value extracted from customers, not created operationally. Roll-ups work, but the base rate is bad and the mechanism is often pricing power, not efficiency. |
| **A0.4** | 🔴 **This one inverts the whole thesis. See below.** AI companies transact at a **13.1x median revenue multiple** in actual M&A; defensible ones 8–12x revenue; narrative-only 3–5x. Consulting firms: 4–7x EBITDA. An electrical contractor: 5–8x EBITDA ≈ **0.5–0.8x revenue.** |

### The A0.4 problem, stated plainly

**A dollar of revenue inside a defensible AI services business is worth roughly 10–15x more than a dollar of revenue inside an electrical contractor.**

I spent eight rounds recommending that the owner of an AI services business go and buy assets where revenue is valued at ~1/15th the rate. That comparison was never once made.

It does not automatically mean "don't buy the contractor" — the multiple gap partly reflects durability risk, and Kenius may not be at defensible scale. But it does mean the alternative was never priced, and it should have been on page one.

## Three traps I walked into

### 1. Complexity trap — and it's fatal to my own conclusion

My final recommendation: buy a commercial electrical contractor at 4–5x, shift end-market mix toward data-centre/mission-critical (+1–2 turns), convert project revenue to contracted maintenance (+0.5–0.8 turns), exit at 8–12x.

**Remove one piece: remove the AI. Does the core thesis still work?**

**Yes. Entirely.** Both value-creation levers — mix shift and maintenance conversion — are ordinary contracting strategy. Neither requires a language model. **The AI was not load-bearing in my own final answer.**

Which means one of two things is true, and I think both are:
- the "AI roll-up" frame was the wrong frame, and
- my answer quietly drifted off the question over eight rounds while appearing to converge.

If the best version of the plan doesn't need AI, then the honest recommendation is *"buy a commercial electrical contractor"* — a 1990s private equity strategy — and everything about AI was decoration.

### 2. Analogy trap

I used Long Lake ($100M EBITDA in under two years), Crete/Current, and Crescendo as proof the model works. What problem were *they* solving? Deploying General Catalyst's $1.5B Creation fund and Thrive's $1B — with an OpenAI equity partnership and ex-Palantir operators.

That is not the problem "one person who owns an AI consultancy wants to build wealth." Capital, team, deal access, model access and time horizon all differ. I flagged this once in Part I and then recommended the strategy anyway.

### 3. Legacy trap

The opening message was *"i wanna do ai roll up of smbs."* That framing comes from 2025–26 VC and X discourse. **I never asked why, or what the underlying goal was.** Eight rounds of increasingly precise research on top of an unexamined problem statement — which is the single most expensive kind of error available.
