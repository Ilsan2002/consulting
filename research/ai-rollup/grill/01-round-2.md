### First consequence: this was never a roll-up

At $300k–$1.5M of equity with no SBA leverage, the buyable target is **one small business** — a single location, owner-operated, $150k–$800k of earnings. Not a platform. Not a bolt-on programme. Not ten companies.

**Every part of this research programme from Part III onward was scoped to a buyer who doesn't exist here.** The census of AI roll-ups, the independent-sponsor economics, the $3M-EBITDA platform model, the electrical-contractor mix-shift thesis — all assumed institutional or $5M+ capital. The correct question was always: *"what is the one small US business to buy?"*

## The finding that reframes everything: E-2 and AI margin expansion are in direct conflict

E-2 requirements, verified:

- **Marginality is the #1 cause of E-2 denials in 2026.** The business must do more than support you and your family — it must make a "significant economic contribution."
- **You must employ US workers.** A one-person self-employment model "will likely be rejected."
- **Renewal, indefinitely, is conditional** on the enterprise continuing to operate successfully — you must show **evidence of both revenue and job creation.**
- **You must "develop and direct"** the enterprise, in person. Passive ownership fails.

Now set that against the thesis this entire programme has been built on:

> **The AI roll-up thesis is: buy a labour-intensive service business and use AI to need fewer people.**

> **The E-2 visa is: keep this business, and keep employing Americans, or you lose your status.**

**These are structurally opposed.** On an E-2, headcount reduction is not a value-creation lever — it is an immigration risk. Every margin story in Parts I–VII assumed you could shrink the payroll. You cannot.

### What survives, and it's the better thesis anyway

AI has two possible roles. This programme only ever considered the first:

| | Lever | E-2 compatible? |
|---|---|---|
| ❌ | **Cost:** same revenue, fewer people | **No** — jeopardises renewal, and Part I established this saving gets competed away regardless |
| ✅ | **Growth:** more revenue per person, headcount grows anyway | **Yes** — and it satisfies marginality, job creation, and renewal simultaneously |

**The constraint forces you onto the better lever.** Part V, Finding 5 (the Jevons argument) already established that where price or capacity was the binding constraint, cutting cost multiplies volume rather than transferring margin. On an E-2 you *must* run that version — grow revenue faster than headcount, and let headcount grow.

That is also the honest answer to **"AI future-proof."** You are not future-proofed by owning something AI can't touch — that is a bet against the trend and it decays. You are future-proofed by owning the **scarce complement that gets more valuable as intelligence gets cheaper** (Part VII), while using AI to take share from competitors who don't.

## Consequence for the pick: electrical contracting is now probably wrong

It was the right answer for a $5M-equity institutional buyer. Against the actual constraint set:

| Constraint | Electrical contracting |
|---|---|
| $0.75–4M EV | Buys a very small shop — below the scale where the mix-shift thesis operates |
| Data-centre mix shift | Requires bonding, project capability and scale a sub-$1M-EBITDA shop doesn't have |
| **Licence** | **In Colorado the firm must be owned wholly/partly by a master electrician; in Minnesota the responsible individual must be an owner, officer or member.** A first-time foreign owner may have to hand equity to the qualifier — who then holds the business hostage |
| E-2 "develop and direct" | You'd be directing a licensed trade you cannot practise, with the licence held by someone else |
| Cyclicality | Commercial construction turns; E-2 renewal doesn't care that it was the cycle |

**Verdict: discard for this buyer.** Keep it on file as the right answer for a different, better-capitalised buyer.
